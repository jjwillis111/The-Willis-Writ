Law journal note

From the Seas to the Stars

Adapting UNCLOS as a Model for Equitable Space Resource Governance · International Law Journal, tentatively slated for spring 2027

The 1967 Outer Space Treaty prohibits national appropriation of outer space but says little about private extraction of space resources, and national laws in the United States, Luxembourg, and Japan now permit it.

My Note argues that the 1982 Convention on the Law of the Sea, and the licensing, environmental review, and dispute resolution system of the International Seabed Authority it created, offer a model for an International Space Resources Authority. I researched and drafted it independently.

The proposed authority

  1. Structure. An Assembly of all member States, a thirty-six member Council elected from five groups, an independent Secretariat, and an Independent Environmental and Technical Commission that reviews every license application in a public process.
  2. Licensing. Three tiers scaled to risk: survey and prospecting, extraction, and large-scale commercial operations. Royalties are exempt at Tier 1, three percent of gross revenue at Tier 2, and five to eight percent at Tier 3.
  3. Environment. Impact assessments before any extraction, and Space Resource Conservation Zones where extraction is prohibited.

Disputes and enforcement

  1. Disputes. Negotiation and conciliation first, then a permanent Tribunal of eleven jurists, with a Technical Panel for licensing and environmental questions.
  2. Enforcement. Financial assurance bonds forfeited on material non-compliance, sponsoring State responsibility, and continuous data sharing that allows remote monitoring.
  3. Certainty. I argue that a single licensing regime recognized by all member States gives operators more legal certainty than a patchwork of national laws.

Research

Empirical work on dispute settlement reform

UNCITRAL Working Group III

Research Assistant to Professor Weijia Rao, with co-author Niccolò Ridi · Summer 2026

An empirical study of how states have positioned themselves in the Working Group's deliberations on reform of investor-state dispute settlement (ISDS).

I coded delegates' statements from the Working Group's sessions, matched them to the reform propositions in the study, flagged expressions of support, and grouped the remaining claims by theme.

Method

The coding workbook, built in Python, has four sheets.

  1. The reform propositions from the report.
  2. The claims made at each meeting, quoted verbatim.
  3. A match of each claim to a proposition.
  4. A thematic clustering of the claims that match no proposition.

Following developments

Alongside this project I keep up with regulatory and policy developments at the intersection of law, technology and public policy. Legal Wire, the newspaper behind the globe on the shelf, collects the headlines I follow.

Open Legal Wire

What it taught me

Working with Professor Rao, I helped develop a strict coding protocol: quote the source verbatim, re-audit line by line, and never trust a partial read of a transcript.

That habit of disciplined source work carries directly into legal research and into any investigation where the record has to hold up.
